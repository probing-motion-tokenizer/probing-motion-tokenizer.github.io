Accurate body motion does not, by itself, guarantee that a tokenizer preserves communicatively important details.

Abstract

Discrete motion tokenizers encode motion as atomic units and are widely used for co-speech gesture generation. It remains unclear which motion properties, especially those relevant to gesture semantics, are recoverable from these codebooks. We probe a reconstruction-trained codebook using 19 co-speech gesture descriptors spanning from raw motion to abstract communicative function. Results show that geometry and handedness are readily decodable from token embeddings, while motion category is only weakly decoded despite showing systematic differences in discrete code usage. This gap between reconstruction quality and descriptor decodability suggests that reconstruction objectives alone do not guarantee that gesture semantics are captured, and that evaluating codebooks on such properties can guide the design of more semantic motion tokenizers.

Probing embeddings and discrete code usage

Can a descriptor be decoded from pooled token embeddings? Do its classes use different discrete codes? We examine both questions.

Model and evaluation details

Our main analysis uses the model with the lowest reconstruction error: latent dimension 256, codebook size 2,048, and temporal downsampling factor 4. It is trained on combined BEAT and TED motion using position reconstruction and VQ commitment losses.

The body stream contains 8 coarse joints and the hand stream 30 finger joints; a shared decoder reconstructs the full pose. The configuration sweep contains 27 models. Probing is complemented by a comparison with EMAGE and by correlations between macro-F1 and mean per-joint position error (MPJPE).

The linear probe is multinomial logistic regression with balanced class weights; the MLP has one 128-unit ReLU layer. Both use the same five folds and class balancing. Code-level separation is tested with label permutations and Benjamini–Hochberg correction across descriptors within each stream. MI is the sum of the mutual information between each code’s presence and the descriptor labels. For descriptor-level code-usage results, we report the stream with the highest MI for each descriptor; q-values refer to the corrected JS permutation tests.

Fine hand details are less faithfully reconstructed

Our qualitative examples show that upper-body geometry is preserved while fine-grained hand poses are averaged out. We attribute this gap in reconstruction quality to the difference in scale between the hands and the upper body.

Evaluate beyond reconstruction

Good reconstruction alone is not a sufficient measure of semantic accessibility.

Use both descriptor probes and code-level analyses to examine what a motion tokenizer preserves.

What our findings suggest

Future tokenizers should explicitly preserve communicatively relevant properties. The agreement between two architectures suggests that the observed gradient is not specific to one tokenizer design.

Limitations

Our analysis is limited to 778 TED clips and two skeleton-based, reconstruction-trained tokenizers. The findings may not generalize to other domains, motion representations or training objectives. We did not test the separate contributions of BEAT and TED, and manual annotations may be sparse or subjective. Low probe performance does not establish that a property is absent or inaccessible to a sequence-aware model.
